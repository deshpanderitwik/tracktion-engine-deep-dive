DAW conceptual map

Every mature DAW has roughly these ten concepts. The names differ between DAWs; the concepts don't. This diagram is the vocabulary for every later page — open it, read it, and come back when a later diagram uses a word you haven't met yet.

Ten nouns

A DAW is software that lets one person play the role of an entire recording studio. That ambition decomposes into roughly ten concepts. At the top is the Edit: one document, one musical piece. Every other concept on this page lives inside an Edit. It holds a tree of Tracks, where each track is a lane for one instrument or one voice. A track owns Clips — the bits of music arranged along the timeline. A clip is either a reference into an AudioFile (the WAV of a guitar take) or a bag of MIDI notes (a programmed drum pattern). Scenes group clips across tracks so you can launch them together, Ableton-style. Markers pin points on the timeline. A TempoSequence says how fast the music is going at any point along its length. Automation is any parameter value that changes over time.

Describing vs. playing

The Edit describes. The Edit does not play. For that you need the Transport: play, stop, record, loop. Transport owns a PlayHead — the cursor that says "the current time is here, moving this fast." When Transport starts, the engine builds an AudioGraph: a directed graph of processing nodes derived from the Edit's structure. Audio samples flow block-by-block through that graph and out to an AudioDevice. Individual Plugins (an EQ, a reverb, a synth) are nodes in this graph. A Rack is a plugin that is secretly a sub-graph of other plugins — a modular synth patch living inside one plugin slot.

Press play and three lanes light up at once. A single gesture fires Transport (the playback lane), which schedules the AudioGraph (the signal lane), which reads clip data from Tracks (the arrangement lane) and emits audio to the AudioDevice (the I/O lane). The press play flow overlay in the sidebar traces that exact path. Toggle it on and you'll see which edges light up — the shape of a single user gesture crossing four subsystems.

The one interesting tradeoff

Two models run at the same time. The arrangement model is offline and editable — you can nudge a clip or change a tempo with no audio implications until the next block. The graph model is realtime and strict — the audio thread may not allocate, lock, or block, ever. Turning the first into the second, safely, on every change, sample-accurately, is the single hardest problem in the engine. Almost every later diagram is a detail of that translation.

What's flagged

The red circles in the sidebar mark places where a concept on this diagram hides a complication that deserves its own later page. Rack's dual identity (flag 2) comes back as diagram 6. Automation's offline-to-realtime boundary (flag 3) is diagram 11. The split between a DAG scheduler and engine-specific nodes (flag 4) drives diagrams 2 and 4. And nested Edits (flag 1) — a Clip that is itself an Edit — is a recursion that shows up wherever "freeze" or "bounce" appears in the source.

A word on the diagram grammar you're seeing for the first time. Solid edges are ownership ("Edit contains Tracks"). Dashed edges are runtime messages ("Transport tells PlayHead to start"). Dotted edges are data flows ("audio samples from Clip move through the graph"). Every later diagram keeps those three meanings and those three meanings only.
